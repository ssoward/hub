# Analysis: 1 Nephi 4 × "Coming Closer to Jesus Christ"
**1 Nephi 4** (Jerusalem, about 600–592 B.C.) cross-referenced with **President Dallin H. Oaks**, "Coming Closer to Jesus Christ," BYU Devotional, February 10, 2026.

---

## The Cross-Reference in One Sentence

Nephi's question outside the walls of Jerusalem — *"wherefore can ye doubt?"* (1 Ne. 4:3) — is the question President Oaks answers 2,600 years later, and the four practices Oaks prescribes for overcoming doubt (strengthen faith, increase humility, seek help from believers, be patient with God's timing) are each already enacted, in narrative form, inside the chapter.

---

## The Two Texts

| | 1 Nephi 4 | "Coming Closer to Jesus Christ" |
|---|---|---|
| **Setting** | Outside and inside Jerusalem's walls, by night, about 600 B.C. | Marriott Center, BYU devotional, February 10, 2026 |
| **Speaker** | Nephi, recording events decades later on the small plates | Dallin H. Oaks, newly President of the Church, in his first devotional after receiving the mantle |
| **Occasioned by** | Two failed attempts to obtain the plates of brass (1 Ne. 3) | Members' doubts about doctrine, history, and social issues |
| **Governing question** | "Wherefore can ye doubt?" (v. 3) | "Whatever those doubts, the way to overcome them is to get closer to our Savior Jesus Christ." |
| **Resolution** | Obedience to the voice of the Spirit, sealed by covenant (vv. 18, 35–38) | Four practices that draw a disciple closer to Christ |

President Oaks opened with the mantle of Elijah passing to Elisha (2 Kings 2) — prophetic authority carried across a succession. It is worth noticing that 1 Nephi 4 is itself a succession story: the plates of brass pass out of the custody of a corrupt record-keeper into the hands of a covenant family, so that the word of God can cross an ocean and survive six hundred years. Both texts are about how the word and the authority of God get carried forward by imperfect people who act in faith.

## Structure of 1 Nephi 4 — Five Movements

| | Verses | Movement | What it models |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1–3 | Nephi rallies his brothers | Faith built on remembered evidence |
| 2 | 4–9 | He creeps into the city alone | Acting without a script |
| 3 | 10–18 | The threefold constraint at Laban's body | Humility under hard direction |
| 4 | 19–29 | The stratagem in Laban's garments | Boldness after submission |
| 5 | 30–38 | Zoram's oath and the departure | Covenant community |

## Primary Findings

### 1. Nephi's answer to doubt is memory, and Oaks' first practice is the same discipline.

Nephi does not argue his brothers out of doubt with new information. He rehearses what they already know:

> "Let us be strong like unto Moses; for he truly spake unto the waters of the Red Sea and they divided… Now behold ye know that this is true; and ye also know that an angel hath spoken unto you; wherefore can ye doubt?" (vv. 2–3)

Two evidences — one scriptural (the Exodus), one personal (the angel of chapter 3, which Laman and Lemuel saw with their own eyes). The logic is: *God's demonstrated past power is the ground for present confidence.* "The Lord is able to deliver us, **even as** our fathers, and to destroy Laban, **even as** the Egyptians" (v. 3). Faith reasons by precedent.

Oaks' first practice is the maintenance of exactly this memory: "Strong faith requires more than strong desire. It means daily trying, one step at a time, with prayer and scripture study." Scripture study *is* the rehearsal of God's precedents. And his anchor — "Hold on to the first article of faith, even if something else is uncertain" — is structurally identical to Nephi's move: hold the established thing while the uncertain thing is still uncertain.

The sobering footnote: Laman and Lemuel had *seen an angel* and still doubted. Evidence alone does not produce faith; what is done with evidence does. That is why Oaks prescribes practices rather than proofs.

### 2. "Not knowing beforehand" (v. 6) is the verse President Nelson's warning was built for.

> "And I was led by the Spirit, not knowing beforehand the things which I should do." (v. 6)

This is the most quoted verse of the chapter and the hinge of the cross-reference. Oaks re-issued President Nelson's warning: "in coming days, it will not be possible to survive spiritually without the guiding, directing, comforting, and constant influence of the Holy Ghost," and added his own gloss: "the adversary has become so effective at disguising truth that if you don't have the Holy Ghost, you will be deceived."

Nephi in Jerusalem is the case study of that survival. He has no plan — two plans have already failed (the lot, the treasure). What he has instead is a *connection*: real-time guidance that only discloses the next step. Verse 6 defines what "constant influence" actually feels like from the inside: not a briefing, but a leading.

Note the order of verses 5–6: Nephi is already creeping toward Laban's house *before* he says he was led not knowing beforehand. Revelation in this chapter comes to someone already in motion. Oaks' corresponding counsel: "while you wait, wait upon the Lord… and while we wait, we should busy ourselves with service." Waiting on the Lord, in both texts, is done on your feet.

### 3. "I shrunk" is humility as teachableness — Oaks' second practice in narrative form.

The Spirit constrains Nephi three times (vv. 10, 11–13), and Nephi's first response is recoil:

> "I said in my heart: Never at any time have I shed the blood of man. And I shrunk and would that I might not slay him." (v. 10)

Read against Oaks, the shrinking is not a flaw in Nephi's faith — it is the *teachable pause*. President Kimball, quoted by Oaks: "Humility is teachableness — an ability to realize that all virtues and abilities are not concentrated in one's self." President Benson, also quoted: "The antidote for pride is humility — meekness, submissiveness."

Nephi does neither of the two easy things. He does not obey instantly and thoughtlessly, and he does not harden into refusal. He stays in the conversation — voices his objection ("in my heart"), keeps listening, and lets the Spirit teach him. Oaks: "When we are humble, we can more clearly hear the Lord's voice." Verses 10–18 are eight verses of increasingly clear hearing.

### 4. Nephi resolves the hardest command by reasoning from covenant promises — Oaks' "spiritual method" exactly.

Watch the anatomy of verses 14–17. The Spirit has spoken twice; what finally moves Nephi is *remembered scripture*:

> "I remembered the words of the Lord which he spake unto me in the wilderness, saying that: Inasmuch as thy seed shall keep my commandments, they shall prosper in the land of promise." (v. 14)

From that remembered promise Nephi reasons: the seed cannot keep commandments they do not have (v. 15); the law is engraven on the plates of brass (v. 16); therefore the Lord's deliverance of Laban has a purpose — the records (v. 17). Only then: "Therefore I did obey the voice of the Spirit" (v. 18).

Oaks described "two methods of gaining needed knowledge": the scientific method, and "the truths disclosed by the spiritual method, which begins with faith in God and relies on scriptures, inspired teaching, and personal revelation." Verses 10–18 run the spiritual method step by step: personal revelation (the Spirit's words) + scripture (the remembered covenant) + reasoning within faith → settled conviction → action. Nephi is not credulous; he is *convinced*, and the text preserves the machinery of his convincing.

### 5. The Laban problem is a live example of the doubts Oaks addresses — and the chapter itself supplies Oaks' toolkit.

Honesty requires naming it: the slaying of Laban is one of the most ethically difficult passages in the Book of Mormon, and it has troubled careful readers from the beginning. A cross-reference with a talk about *overcoming doubt* cannot skip the fact that this chapter *raises* the kind of doubt the talk is about.

Three observations:

- **The text preserves the difficulty on purpose.** Nephi, writing decades later, could have omitted his recoil. Instead he records "I shrunk" and his own objection. The narrator wants the reader to know this was hard — that the difficulty is not a modern discovery but the original experience.
- **The justifications are given, and they are covenantal, not casual.** Delivered into thy hands (twice); he sought thy life; he robbed you; he would not hearken (vv. 11–12); and the stated principle: "It is better that one man should perish than that a nation should dwindle and perish in unbelief" (v. 13). The stakes named are not property but *faith itself* — a nation's access to the word of God.
- **Oaks' four practices are the sanctioned way to sit with such a passage.** "Overcoming doubt… can be a lengthy process, as with building faith or acquiring humility." "Don't be persuaded by false or inaccurate information. Discuss your concerns with faithful, well-informed friends and always take those concerns to the Lord." "Hold on to the first article of faith, even if something else is uncertain." The reader troubled by verse 18 is invited to do precisely what Nephi did at verse 10: stay in the conversation.

### 6. Laban is the portrait of proximity without discipleship.

Laban *possessed* the plates of brass — the scriptures, the genealogy, the law — and it did him no good at all: "he would not hearken unto the commandments of the Lord" (v. 11). He sat closer to the word of God than anyone in Jerusalem and was further from its Author than anyone in the chapter. He is Benson's pride sketch in one figure: property, position, "his fifty," wine, and no submissiveness anywhere.

Oaks' warning lands on the same distinction: "Some may protest or question the truth of Church doctrine without knowing or even understanding the fulness of that doctrine." Access to religious material — a treasury full of it — is not the same as coming closer to Jesus Christ.

### 7. Zoram is Oaks' third practice — "surround yourself with people who believe" — dramatized.

Oaks told of a stake president who said to a wavering young man, *seek friends and associates who are striving to follow the Lord*, and the young man replied, "I have not heard anyone speak like that in months. I have been hanging out with friends who don't believe."

Zoram's arc is that story in 600 B.C. He spends verses 20–27 walking beside Nephi "concerning the elders of the Jews" — Laban's nighttime circle — and then, outside the walls, hears believing speech for perhaps the first time: "Surely the Lord hath commanded us to do this thing" (v. 34). The text's response verb is exact:

> "And it came to pass that Zoram did take courage **at the words which I spake**." (v. 35)

Words spoken by believers produce courage in the hearer. Zoram trades the household of a man who "would not hearken" for a covenant camp in the wilderness, and the exchange is sealed the way Oaks says spiritual safety is always sealed — by covenant: Nephi's oath (v. 33), Zoram's oath (v. 35), and the immediate fruit, "our fears did cease concerning him" (v. 37). Mutual covenant replaces mutual fear. Oaks' modern equivalents are explicit: "Protect yourself with the weekly renewing of your covenants by partaking of the sacrament… In sum, stay on the covenant path."

And the freedom offered is worth quoting: "he should be a **free man like unto us** if he would go down in the wilderness with us" (v. 33). The wilderness with covenants is freedom; the city without them was servitude.

### 8. Three men, three trajectories.

| | Relation to the word of God | Posture | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Laban** | Custodian of the plates | "Would not hearken"; pride, property, wine | Destruction |
| **Nephi** | Seeker of the plates | Led by the Spirit; shrinks, listens, reasons, obeys | Deliverance and the record |
| **Zoram** | Servant beside the plates | Hears believing words; takes courage; covenants | Freedom — "a free man like unto us" |

The chapter is a controlled experiment: the same night, the same city, the same plates — and the variable that determines each outcome is exactly the variable of Oaks' talk: nearness to the Lord, expressed as hearkening, humility, and covenant.

## Oaks' Four Practices, Mapped onto the Chapter

| Oaks' practice | Where 1 Nephi 4 enacts it |
|---|---|
| **1. Strengthen faith** — daily prayer and scripture study; anchor in the first article of faith | vv. 1–3: Nephi rehearses the Exodus and the angel; v. 14: remembered scripture is what finally settles him |
| **2. Increase humility** — teachableness, meekness, submissiveness | vv. 10–18: "I shrunk" → listens → reasons → "I did obey the voice of the Spirit" |
| **3. Seek help from believers** — avoid unbelieving voices; stay on the covenant path | vv. 4–5: the believing company waits outside the walls; vv. 30–37: Zoram takes courage at believing words and covenants in |
| **4. Be patient — trust God's timing** | v. 6: "not knowing beforehand"; and behind the whole chapter, two failed attempts in 1 Nephi 3 that were not evidence of abandonment but of process |

## The Deepest Common Thread

Verse 13 names the catastrophe the whole chapter exists to prevent: a nation that would "**dwindle and perish in unbelief**." Dwindling — slow, generational loss of faith for lack of the word — is the enemy in 1 Nephi 4, and it is the same enemy in the 2026 devotional: members drifting from belief amid "an abundance of speculation and false information."

The remedies also converge. Nephi secures *scripture* for a covenant people at extraordinary cost; Oaks asks a covenant people to actually open it, daily. The plates Nephi carried out of Jerusalem are, in the economy of the Book of Mormon, the reason his nation had the law, the prophets, and the words that "enlarge the memory" (Alma 37:8). The book bought at that price anchors the practices Oaks prescribes.

Nephi's question and Oaks' thesis are finally one sentence apart:

> "Wherefore can ye doubt? … the Lord is able to deliver us." (1 Ne. 4:3)

> "Whatever those doubts, the way to overcome them is to get closer to our Savior Jesus Christ. Again and again, He has taught us that He is the way."

## Cautions for Teaching

- **Do not flatten the Laban episode.** The chapter is not a proof-text that spiritual promptings routinely override moral law. It is a singular, heavily-justified, covenant-framed exception whose difficulty the narrator deliberately preserves. Teach the shrinking, not just the sword.
- **Verse 6 is not improvisation.** "Not knowing beforehand" followed total commitment ("I will go and do," 1 Ne. 3:7) and two costly attempts. It describes the experience of a disciple already obedient, not a shortcut around preparation.
- **The Oaks talk is a devotional address**, given February 10, 2026 — doctrinally weighty, from the President of the Church, but pastoral in register. Its four practices are counsel, offered as the tested path, not a checklist that mechanically dissolves every question.
- **Date the sources.** 1 Nephi 4: about 600–592 B.C., written retrospectively on the small plates (after 570 B.C.). Oaks: BYU Marriott Center, in the university's 150th year, his first devotional after the mantle passed to him — which is why the talk opens with Elijah and Elisha.
