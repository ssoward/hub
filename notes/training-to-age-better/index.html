Findings

In interview order

1. Path into science

  • Grew up in San Diego.
  • Around 17: surfing, “nature girl,” David Attenborough documentaries.
  • First plan: marine / field biology — Galápagos, animals, ocean.
  • Chose science to get a biology degree, then picked “the hardest thing”: chemistry and biochemistry.
  • Joke against herself: she never left the lab and never became a field biologist.

Lithgow’s parallel: he wanted to be a field biologist — a birdwatcher — in a Scottish steel town; teachers steered him to microbiology.

2. Lab years

First internship (college junior, ~2 years): synthesizing peptides and organic chemistry. She did not enjoy it. Too far from biology.

Salk Institute: an aging lab, C. elegans under the microscope. Her first real excitement about aging came from genetically manipulating worms so they lived about 100% longer, using genes homologous to human genes. Hands-on experiments.

Graduate work: she left worms for cell biology, cancer biology, and cancer metabolism; animal work shifted from worm to mouse. Years on mitochondrial metabolism, including long stretches at the electron microscope. She still calls herself a “mitochondriac,” but with a bigger-picture focus now.

Why leave the worm: partly circumstance, partly a sense that staying only in worm genetics would be too narrow. She wanted systems biology and a wider toolkit — genetics and aging in worms, then metabolism in graduate school, then human metabolism and how to run and read clinical nutrition trials.

Graduate school was stressful. In the same years she got serious about health, fitness, and applied science: if she understood a mechanism, she was more willing to change her own behavior.

3. How communication started

  • In free time she wrote in coffee shops, started a blog, and looked for papers on things like vitamin D and omega-3.
  • Trying to lecture family and friends who were not healthy backfired. They took it personally.
  • Sending articles and videos worked better. It was information, not a scolding.
  • That became the start of her science-communication habit.
  • Official podcast launch: 2014, while still a postdoc.

Two jobs she gives the podcast:

  1. Explain why micronutrient insufficiencies can hurt later even if you feel fine now — plus exercise, running, sauna, and related topics.
  2. Act as an idea incubator. Scientists in one field hear another field and try new experiments; her example is vitamin D–receptor people hearing fasting researchers.

Lithgow says one of his current PhD students is in the lab because of her podcasts.

4. Postdoc work: micronutrient bar, DNA damage, vitamin D

Micronutrients, as she defines them here

  • About 40 essential vitamins, minerals, fatty acids, and amino acids the body cannot make.
  • They run metabolism.
  • She estimates on the order of ~22% of enzymes need a micronutrient as a cofactor.
  • Widespread insufficiencies: people are not eating enough of the foods that cover magnesium and other nutrients, and vitamin D is often low because people are not outdoors.

The bar — one project, not the episode topic

  • Ames and colleagues had already developed a micronutrient-rich bar before she arrived.
  • Name in the lab: the CHORI-bar (Children’s Hospital Oakland Research Institute).
  • Her description: micronutrient-dense, with a fiber matrix, meant to mimic whole food — “not just like a supplement,” “the whole package.”
  • Her assay: DNA double-strand breaks in PBMCs from obese trial participants, before and after the bars.
  • Her hypothesis: obesity is linked to DNA damage; poor diets add refined foods and omit nutrients such as magnesium that matter for DNA health and repair.
  • Same period: other nutrition trials, including blueberry extract.

Vitamin D and TPH2 — separate exploratory work

  • Not a vitamin D wet-lab program. She describes literature diving and in-silico work on vitamin D response elements.
  • Gene of interest: tryptophan hydroxylase 2 (TPH2), which converts tryptophan to serotonin in the brain.
  • Argument: the VDRE sequence suggests vitamin D can activate TPH2.
  • Published as review, perspective, and hypothesis papers — evidence-backed, hoping specialists would test them.
  • Clinical frame she used: neurodevelopmental conditions — autism, schizophrenia, ADHD.
  • In this interview she says later in-vitro and in-vivo studies have confirmed that vitamin D can activate TPH2 and affect serotonin.

Vitamin D as “accelerated aging” — an exchange with Lithgow

Lithgow: deficiency raises risk for cancer, neurological disease, and diabetes; it looks like accelerated aging. Vitamin D receptor knockout mice look aged. In worms — which do not normally see vitamin D — adding it extended lifespan, reduced protein aggregation, and limited A-beta toxicity. He is from Scotland, where there is little sun, and calls it a public-health “easy fix.”

Patrick:

  • About 70% of the US is below sufficiency by Endocrine Society criteria: 30 ng/mL.
  • Observational all-cause mortality: she cites 40–60 ng/mL as a good range.
  • Supplements are an easy way to correct deficiency.
  • Overdose and hypercalcemia: she says you generally need hundreds of thousands of IU per day for multiple days. Kidney disease and similar conditions are physician territory.
  • IOM upper tolerable intake: 4,000 IU/day, which she calls reasonable for most people.

5. Hormesis

She leans on Mark Mattson’s framing: humans evolved with intermittent challenges. The examples she lists —

  • Physical activity (hunting, gathering — not Instacart)
  • Food scarcity, or simply not eating
  • Plant compounds — flavonols, polyphenols — as mild chemical stress
  • Heat and cold

Those challenges turn on built-in stress-response genes. After the short stress ends, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects can last and help with ordinary aging: inflammatory cytokines, protein folding and aggregates, stem-cell function.

Dose matters. Too much of the same stress is harmful — all-day exercise with no recovery, extreme caffeine. Hormesis is the left side of that dose-response curve: a little stress, net benefit. Her blunt line is that we were meant to be stressed; the pathways are in the genome.

Lithgow’s worm parallel: heat shock extends C. elegans life. She cites his work — about 15%, as she remembers it in the exchange.

6. Sauna

Personal origin, not a grant:

  • Graduate school, living across from a YMCA.
  • Sauna roughly seven days a week around 2009, before going into the lab.
  • She already knew heat-shock proteins from Andy Dillin’s worm work: chaperones that help keep protein shape and limit aggregation, with some antioxidant activity.
  • What sold her in her own life: less anxiety and better handling of mental stress, including a failed experiment that set her back six months.

The human epidemiology she cites is Jari Laukkanen’s, at the University of Eastern Finland — observational, as she flags. Traditional sauna temperatures in those data run around 174°F, and frequency of 4–7 times a week looked stronger than 2–3 times a week.

Associations she reports from that work. Observational — she does not call it proven causation.

7. Cold, and the overlap among stressors

Heat then cold is common in Finnish sauna culture and Russian banya practice. The overlap she emphasizes: cold also induces heat-shock proteins; plant compounds such as sulforaphane (from Brassica) can too; heat does it most strongly; and local heat can brown fat, a job usually credited to cold.

The cold physiology she walks through:

  1. Norepinephrine rises — an adrenal hormone in plasma, and also a brain neurotransmitter.
  2. In fat and related tissue, that helps turn on UCP1: protons leak, mitochondria “uncouple,” and energy is released as heat rather than ATP, burning glucose or fatty acids.
  3. Anticipatory adaptation: PGC-1α drives mitochondrial biogenesis in adipose tissue, so the next cold exposure can make more heat.
  4. The extra mitochondria make the fat look darker under a microscope — brown fat.

8. Chronic psychosocial stress vs useful stress

Lithgow points to Nikki Holbrook: mild restraint stress in rats was enough to induce molecular chaperones — the same protein-folding machinery relevant to aging and neurodegeneration. He asks Patrick to connect hormesis to psychology: mindfulness, CBT.

Patrick’s thread:

  • Elissa Epel (UCSF, on Patrick’s podcast years earlier) and others: caregiving, money, and relationship stress.
  • Some work links psychosocial stress to endotoxin / LPS leaking from the gut after bacteria are killed; LPS in blood drives inflammation.
  • Injecting LPS into healthy people can produce depressive symptoms versus saline; in one design she cites, EPA (omega-3) blunted both the inflammatory response and the depressive symptoms.
  • Inflammation is not only mood. She cites biomarker work in centenarians and semi- and supercentenarians: suppressed inflammation was the standout correlate of reaching the next longevity stage and keeping cognition. Animal data also treat chronic inflammation as causal in faster aging — not the only cause.

Preconditioning is the practical idea: sauna, running, Peloton, yoga, plant compounds, some fasting — turn on anti-inflammatory and stress-response genes before the unpredictable hit, whether that is traffic, job loss, or illness in the family. You cannot schedule grief. You can be the mouse that got exercise, heat, or sulforaphane before the stroke.

She separates that from meditation and mindfulness, which she treats as another way to handle stress rather than the same thing as HSP induction.

The contrast she and Lithgow share: chronic load — the Epel/Blackburn telomere work in high-stress parents — looks damaging, while acute, dosed stress can look protective. The practical problem is the boundary.

9. Science communication

Lithgow’s question: science is accepted by some people and ignored by others. What is the challenge? Patrick’s answers in this interview:

  • There are good science journalists. There is also sensationalism and bad incentives.
  • Headlines outrun methods: weak stats, no biochemical measures, clinical endpoints with no check that the intervention did anything in the body.
  • Her job, as she sees it: read quality, and put a study in the big picture — mechanism plus human data — not in isolation. Conflicting results often come from methods.
  • Scientists also fail each other with tunnel vision. Her example: a vitamin D trial that never measures vitamin D.
  • Nutrition is not a drug trial. People already have different vitamin D and magnesium levels and different metabolism genes. A drug starts near zero in the body; a nutrient does not.
  • A concrete complaint from ~2014 coverage: RCTs giving 400 IU vitamin D, often without measuring 25(OH)D, then concluding vitamin D “does nothing.” Her rule of thumb: about 1,000 IU raises blood levels roughly 5 ng/mL in people without major metabolism variants. 400 IU is not much.
  • What her team tries to do: cross-disciplinary reading — worm to mouse to molecular to human — and honest accounts of why studies disagree.

Lithgow praises the way she puts papers on screen while interviewing guests.

10. Where she thinks aging research is going

She refuses certainty on a ten-year look. Two areas excite her.

Interrupted / partial cellular reprogramming. Yamanaka factors can push an old adult cell toward an embryonic-like state. Juan Carlos Belmonte and collaborators pulsed the factors so the cell keeps its identity while the epigenetic “program” is wiped; aggregates and other damage go with it, in the picture she describes. That has the potential to reverse some aspects of aging. It is likely not one-and-done, because cells keep aging, and clinical hurdles will take years. This is the line she is most excited about.

Diluting pro-aging plasma factors. Irina and Michael Conboy (UC Berkeley) and related work: from heterochronic parabiosis to therapeutic plasma exchange — diluting whatever in old plasma accelerates aging.

She does not predict a timeline for clinic-ready reversal.

Claims this interview does not make

  • That the CHORI-bar is the point of the conversation
  • That the bar was built around the serotonin paper
  • That eating the bar raises brain serotonin
  • That sauna epidemiology proves causation
  • That vitamin D, magnesium, sauna, or cold is a complete aging therapy
  • That she ran a vitamin D experimental lab that “discovered” TPH2 as a bench finding

Summary

The episode in one paragraph

Patrick tells Lithgow she backed into hard science after a field-biology dream, learned aging in worms, spent years on mitochondria and cancer metabolism, then used Ames-lab nutrition trials — including DNA-break measures after a micronutrient-and-fiber bar — plus hypothesis work on vitamin D and brain TPH2. She left the bench for communication because mechanism changed her own habits, and because family listened to videos more than lectures. The scientific through-line of the episode is hormesis: intermittent exercise, fasting, plant compounds, heat, and cold turn on stress-response and anti-inflammatory programs that may buffer both aging and unpredictable psychosocial stress. She wants nutrition trials that measure the nutrient, and she is most curious about partial reprogramming and plasma dilution as aging-research bets.
