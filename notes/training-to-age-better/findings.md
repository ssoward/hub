- **Episode:** #04, *Training to Age Better* (published December 6, 2022)
- **Host:** Gordon Lithgow
- **Guest:** Rhonda Patrick (FoundMyFitness)

## What the conversation is

Lithgow introduces Patrick as someone who moved from bench science to public science communication: she represents ideas rather than a company or a university, and talks about aging and human health on her podcasts.

The interview then walks through:

1. How she got into science
2. Lab years (worms → cancer/mitochondria → human nutrition trials)
3. Why she started communicating science
4. Micronutrients, the CHORI-bar as one postdoc project, and vitamin D / *TPH2*
5. Hormesis
6. Sauna and cold
7. Chronic psychosocial stress vs useful acute stress
8. Problems in science communication and nutrition trials
9. Where she thinks aging research is heading

The nutrition bar is one example inside the postdoc chapter. It is not the subject of the episode.

## 1. Path into science

- Grew up in San Diego.
- Around 17: surfing, “nature girl,” David Attenborough documentaries.
- First plan: marine / field biology (Galápagos, animals, ocean).
- Chose science to get a biology degree, then picked “the hardest thing”: chemistry and biochemistry.
- Joke against herself: she never left the lab and never became a field biologist.

Lithgow’s parallel: he wanted to be a field biologist (birdwatcher) in a Scottish steel town; teachers steered him to microbiology.

---

## 2. Lab years

**First internship (college junior, ~2 years)**  
Synthesizing peptides / organic chemistry. She did not enjoy it. Too far from biology.

**Salk Institute**  
Aging lab. *C. elegans* under the microscope. First real excitement about aging: genetically manipulating worms so they lived ~100% longer, using genes homologous to human genes. Hands-on experiments.

**Graduate work**  
Left worms for cell biology, cancer biology, and cancer metabolism. Animal work shifted from worm to mouse. Years on mitochondrial metabolism, including long stretches at the electron microscope. She still calls herself a “mitochondriac,” but with a bigger-picture focus now.

**Why leave the worm**  
Partly circumstance. Partly a sense that staying only in worm genetics would be too narrow. She wanted systems biology and a wider toolkit: genetics and aging in worms → metabolism in graduate school → human metabolism and how to run and read clinical nutrition trials.

Graduate school was stressful. In the same years she got serious about health, fitness, and *applied* science: if she understood a mechanism, she was more willing to change her own behavior.

## 3. How communication started

- In free time she wrote in coffee shops, started a blog, and looked for papers on things like vitamin D and omega-3.
- Trying to lecture family and friends who were not healthy backfired. They took it personally.
- Sending articles and videos worked better. It was information, not a scolding.
- That became the start of her science-communication habit.
- Official podcast launch: **2014**, while still a postdoc.
- Two jobs for the podcast:
  1. Explain why micronutrient insufficiencies can hurt later even if you feel fine now; also exercise, running, sauna, and related topics.
  2. Act as an **idea incubator**. Scientists in one field hear another field and try new experiments (example she gives: vitamin D–receptor people hearing fasting researchers).

Lithgow says one of his current PhD students is in the lab because of her podcasts.

## 4. Postdoc work: micronutrient bar, DNA damage, vitamin D

### Micronutrients (her definition in this interview)

- About **40** essential vitamins, minerals, fatty acids, and amino acids the body cannot make.
- They run metabolism.
- She estimates on the order of **~22% of enzymes** need a micronutrient as a cofactor.
- Widespread insufficiencies: people are not eating enough of the foods that cover magnesium and other nutrients; vitamin D is often low because people are not outdoors.

### The bar (one project, not the episode topic)

- Ames and colleagues had already developed a micronutrient-rich bar before she arrived.
- Name in the lab: **CHORI-bar** (Children’s Hospital Oakland Research Institute).
- Her description: micronutrient-dense, with a **fiber matrix**, meant to mimic whole food — “not just like a supplement,” “the whole package.”
- Her assay: **DNA double-strand breaks** in **PBMCs** from **obese** trial participants, before and after the bars.
- Her hypothesis: obesity is linked to DNA damage; poor diets add refined foods *and* omit nutrients such as **magnesium** that matter for DNA health and repair.
- Same period: other nutrition trials, including blueberry extract.

### Vitamin D / *TPH2* (separate exploratory work)

- Not a vitamin D wet-lab program. She describes literature diving and **in-silico** work on vitamin D response elements.
- Gene of interest: **tryptophan hydroxylase 2 (*TPH2*)**, which converts tryptophan to serotonin in the brain.
- Argument: the VDRE sequence suggests vitamin D can activate *TPH2*.
- Published as review / perspective / hypothesis papers, evidence-backed, hoping specialists would test them.
- Clinical frame she used: neurodevelopmental conditions — **autism, schizophrenia, ADHD**.
- In this interview she says later **in-vitro and in-vivo** studies have confirmed that vitamin D can activate *TPH2* and affect serotonin.

### Vitamin D as “accelerated aging” (exchange with Lithgow)

Lithgow: deficiency raises risk for cancer, neurological disease, diabetes; looks like accelerated aging. Vitamin D receptor knockout mice look aged. In worms — which do not normally see vitamin D — adding it extended lifespan, reduced protein aggregation, and limited A-beta toxicity. He is from Scotland; little sun; public-health “easy fix.”

Patrick:

- About **70%** of the US is below sufficiency by Endocrine Society criteria: **30 ng/mL**.
- Observational all-cause mortality: she cites **40–60 ng/mL** as a good range.
- Supplements are an easy way to correct deficiency.
- Overdose / hypercalcemia: she says you generally need **hundreds of thousands of IU per day for multiple days**. Kidney disease and similar conditions are physician territory.
- IOM upper tolerable intake: **4,000 IU/day**, which she calls reasonable for most people.

## 5. Hormesis

She leans on Mark Mattson’s framing: humans evolved with **intermittent challenges**.

Examples she lists:

- Physical activity (hunting, gathering — not Instacart)
- Food scarcity / not eating
- Plant compounds (flavonols, polyphenols) as mild chemical stress
- Heat and cold

Those challenges turn on built-in stress-response genes. After the short stress ends, antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects can last and help with ordinary aging: inflammatory cytokines, protein folding / aggregates, stem-cell function.

Dose matters. Too much of the same stress is harmful (all-day exercise with no recovery; extreme caffeine). Hormesis is the left side of that dose-response curve: a little stress, net benefit.

Her blunt line: we were meant to be stressed; the pathways are in the genome.

Lithgow’s worm parallel: heat shock extends *C. elegans* life. She cites his work (~15% in the exchange as she remembers it).

## 6. Sauna

Personal origin, not a grant:

- Graduate school, lived across from a YMCA.
- Sauna roughly **seven days a week** around **2009**, before going into lab.
- Already knew heat-shock proteins from Andy Dillin’s worm work: chaperones that help keep protein shape and limit aggregation; some antioxidant activity.
- What sold her in her own life: less anxiety and better handling of mental stress, including a failed experiment that set her back six months.

Human epidemiology she cites: **Jari Laukkanen**, University of Eastern Finland.

Associations she reports from that work (observational; she flags that):

- Traditional sauna temperatures in those data ~**174°F**.
- Frequency: **4–7 times/week** vs 1 time/week looked stronger than **2–3 times/week**.
- Figures she gives:
  - Cardiovascular mortality ~**50%** lower at 4–7×/week vs 1×; ~**27%** lower at 2–3× vs 1×
  - ~**11 minutes** even at high frequency: only ~**8%** lower CV mortality
  - ~**20 minutes**: the ~**50%** figure
  - Coronary heart disease mortality ~**68%** lower at 4–7× vs 1×
  - All-cause mortality ~**40%** lower at 4–7× vs 1×
- She treats dose-dependence (frequency *and* duration) plus adjustment for lipids, exercise, socioeconomic status, etc., as more reassuring than a single binary comparison, while still not calling it proven causation.

## 7. Cold (and overlap among stressors)

Common in Finnish sauna culture and Russian banya practice: heat then cold.

Overlap she emphasizes:

- Cold also induces heat-shock proteins.
- Plant compounds such as sulforaphane (Brassica) can too.
- Heat does it most strongly.
- Local heat can brown fat, a job usually credited to cold.

Cold physiology she walks through:

1. **Norepinephrine** rises (adrenal hormone in plasma; also a brain neurotransmitter).
2. In fat (and related tissue), that helps turn on **UCP1**: protons leak, mitochondria “uncouple,” energy is released as **heat** rather than ATP, burning glucose or fatty acids.
3. Anticipatory adaptation: **PGC-1α** → **mitochondrial biogenesis** in adipose tissue, so the next cold exposure can make more heat.
4. Extra mitochondria make the fat look darker under a microscope → **brown fat**.

## 8. Chronic psychosocial stress vs useful stress

Lithgow points to Nikki Holbrook: mild restraint stress in rats was enough to induce molecular chaperones — the same protein-folding machinery relevant to aging and neurodegeneration. He asks Patrick to connect hormesis to psychology (mindfulness, CBT).

Patrick’s thread:

- Elissa Epel (UCSF; on Patrick’s podcast years earlier) and others: caregiving, money, relationship stress.
- Some work links psychosocial stress to **endotoxin / LPS** leaking from the gut after bacteria are killed; LPS in blood drives inflammation.
- Injecting LPS into healthy people can produce depressive symptoms vs saline; in one design she cites, **EPA (omega-3)** blunted the inflammatory response and the depressive symptoms.
- Inflammation is not only mood. She cites biomarker work in centenarians / semi- and supercentenarians: **suppressed inflammation** was the standout correlate of reaching the next longevity stage and keeping cognition. Animal data also treat chronic inflammation as causal in faster aging — not the only cause.

**Preconditioning:** sauna, running, Peloton, yoga, plant compounds, some fasting — turn on anti-inflammatory / stress-response genes *before* the unpredictable hit (traffic, job loss, illness in the family). You cannot schedule grief. You can be the mouse that got exercise, heat, or sulforaphane before the stroke.

She separates that from meditation / mindfulness, which she treats as another way to handle stress, not the same as HSP induction.

Contrast she and Lithgow share: **chronic** load (Epel/Blackburn telomere work in high-stress parents) looks damaging; **acute, dosed** stress can look protective. The practical problem is the boundary.

## 9. Science communication

Lithgow’s question: science is accepted by some people, ignored by others. What is the challenge?

Patrick’s answers in this interview:

- There are good science journalists. There is also sensationalism and bad incentives.
- Headlines outrun methods: weak stats, no biochemical measures, clinical endpoints with no check that the intervention did anything in the body.
- Her job, as she sees it: read quality and put a study in the **big picture** (mechanism + human data), not in isolation. Conflicting results often come from methods.
- Scientists also fail each other with tunnel vision (example: vitamin D trial that never measures vitamin D).
- Nutrition is not a drug trial. People already have different vitamin D and magnesium levels and different metabolism genes. A drug starts near zero in the body; a nutrient does not.
- Concrete complaint from ~2014 coverage: RCTs giving **400 IU** vitamin D, often **without measuring 25(OH)D**, then concluding vitamin D “does nothing.” Her rule of thumb here: ~**1,000 IU** raises blood levels about **5 ng/mL** in people without major metabolism variants. 400 IU is not much.
- What her team tries to do: cross-disciplinary reading (worm → mouse → molecular → human) and honest accounts of why studies disagree.

Lithgow praises the way she puts papers on screen while interviewing guests.

## 10. Where she thinks aging research is going (~10-year look)

She refuses certainty. Two areas she is excited about:

**Interrupted / partial cellular reprogramming**  
Yamanaka factors can push an old adult cell toward an embryonic-like state. Juan Carlos Belmonte and collaborators pulsed the factors so the cell **keeps its identity** but the epigenetic “program” is wiped; aggregates and other damage go with it, in the picture she describes. Potential to reverse some aging aspects. Likely not one-and-done, because cells keep aging. Clinical hurdles will take years. This is the line she is **most** excited about.

**Diluting pro-aging plasma factors**  
Irina and Michael Conboy (UC Berkeley) and related work: from heterochronic parabiosis to **therapeutic plasma exchange** / dilution of whatever in old plasma accelerates aging.

She does not predict a timeline for clinic-ready reversal.

- That sauna epidemiology proves causation
- That vitamin D, magnesium, sauna, or cold is a complete aging therapy
- That she ran a vitamin D experimental lab that “discovered” *TPH2* as a bench finding

## Short abstract (interview-shaped)

Patrick tells Lithgow she backed into hard science after a field-biology dream, learned aging in worms, spent years on mitochondria and cancer metabolism, then used Ames-lab nutrition trials — including DNA-break measures after a micronutrient-and-fiber bar — plus hypothesis work on vitamin D and brain *TPH2*. She left the bench for communication because mechanism changed her own habits and because family listened to videos more than lectures. The scientific through-line of the episode is **hormesis**: intermittent exercise, fasting, plant compounds, heat, and cold turn on stress-response and anti-inflammatory programs that may buffer both aging and unpredictable psychosocial stress. She wants nutrition trials that measure the nutrient, and she is most curious about partial reprogramming and plasma dilution as aging-research bets.
